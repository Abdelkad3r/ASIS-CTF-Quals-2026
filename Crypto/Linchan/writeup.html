The whole challenge in one distribution

Real subspaces are seeded with two matrices of rank exactly 25. Decoy subspaces are uniform. This is how far out on the tail rank 25 sits:

A decoy subspace of dimension 18 holds 218 matrices, so its expected number of rank-25 elements is 1.6 × 10-9. Across all 102 decoys, about 10-7. Every low-rank matrix you find is planted.

01

Reading through the obfuscation

The generator uses single-letter names throughout, but all of it is linear algebra over GF(2), with a 32×32 matrix stored as 32 integers.

NameMeaning
_rrank of a set of vectors
_m, _t, _imatrix multiply, transpose, inverse
_g()uniform invertible 32×32 matrix
_h()32×25 times 25×32 → a matrix of rank exactly 25
_b(m, q)basis of an m-dimensional subspace; if q, the first two elements come from _h()
_o(B)re-express in a random basis, then transpose everything with probability ½
_f(S)canonical form: min over {S, S⁻¹, Sᵀ, S⁻ᵀ}
_k(K)SHAKE-256 over the sorted canonical forms of the secrets
02

The scheme

_l = ((16, 2), (17, 2), (18, 1))     # (dimension, number of real pairs)
_d = 34                              # decoys per dimension

for m, c in _l:
    for _ in range(c):
        C, S = _b(m, True), _g()          # True -> plant two rank-25 matrices
        D = [_m(_m(S, A), _i(S)) for A in C]  # D = S C S^-1
        B += [(m, _o(C)), (m, _o(D))]
        K.append(S)
    for _ in range(_d):
        B.append((m, _o(_b(m))))              # decoy
shuffle(B)

The output is 112 boxes, each an m-dimensional subspace of the 1024-dimensional space of 32×32 matrices, handed over in a random basis and transposed half the time. Ten of them form five conjugate pairs; the flag is encrypted under a key derived from the five secret S.

What the key actually needs

_f quotients out {S, S⁻¹, Sᵀ, S⁻ᵀ} and the forms are sorted before hashing. Recovering any representative of each class, in any order, is enough — which turns out to matter in step 08.

03

The problem as intended

You get two subspaces and the promise that one is a conjugate of the other — but not which box pairs with which, not the basis correspondence, and not whether either has been transposed. Solving X · span(C) · X⁻¹ = span(D) head-on is bilinear: you would have to find X and the m×m change of basis relating the two spanning sets simultaneously.

The decoys sharpen it. With 38 boxes at one dimension there are 703 candidate pairings, so any per-pair test has to be cheap.

04

Why the seeding leaks

Two properties make the rank anomaly at the top of this page fatal rather than merely curious:

  • Rank belongs to the subspace, not to the basis. _o() re-expresses the basis through a random invertible change of basis. The set of elements is untouched, so the planted matrices are still in there.
  • Rank survives both conjugation and transposition. rank(S H S⁻¹) = rank(H) and rank(Hᵀ) = rank(H), so the marker reappears in the partner box and survives _o()'s coin flip.

Finding a low-rank element of a matrix subspace is the MinRank problem, which is hard — asymptotically, in the dimension of the span. Here m ≤ 18.

05

MinRank by exhaustion

38 × (2^16 - 1) + 38 × (2^17 - 1) + 36 × (2^18 - 1) = 16,908,176 matrices

A short C helper walks each subspace in Gray-code order, so stepping to the next element costs one XOR of 32 words, and ranks each with a 32-step elimination.

[+] 112 boxes, 16,908,176 subspace elements to scan
[+] MinRank: 20 matrices of rank <= 26 in 10 boxes
[+] real boxes by dimension: {16: [49, 60, 82, 86], 17: [1, 47, 92, 106], 18: [32, 44]}

Fourteen seconds, and exactly as predicted: 20 hits, all of rank exactly 25, two per box, in ten boxes, splitting 4 / 4 / 2 across the dimensions — the shape of _l. No false positives.

06

Pairing the boxes

Ten real boxes still need matching into five pairs. Conjugate matrices are similar, so any similarity invariant works — provided it also survives transposition. The rank sequence of the powers does both:

fingerprint(H) = tuple(rank(H**k) for k in range(1, 13))

Since rank((S H S⁻¹)ᵏ) = rank(Hᵏ) and rank((Hᵏ)ᵀ) = rank(Hᵏ), a real pair has matching fingerprint multisets and nothing else does.

07

Recovering S is then linear

Here the planted matrices pay off a second time. They are not only a marker — they are canonically identifiable inside their own subspace, being its only rank-25 elements. That removes the unknown change of basis entirely: up to swapping the two, Gᵢ = S Hᵢ S⁻¹.

The unknown is now only S, and the relation is linear in it:

X · H₁ = G₁ · X
X · H₂ = G₂ · X

entry (i, j) of each, over the 1024 bits of X:
sum_k H[k][j] · X[i][k]   xor   sum_k G[i][k] · X[k][j]   =   0

That is 2048 equations in 1024 unknowns over GF(2) — one Gaussian elimination. The solution space is S · Centralizer(H₁, H₂), and two random rank-25 matrices generate the whole algebra M₃₂(GF(2)), whose centralizer is just {0, I}. So the space should be one-dimensional and its single nonzero element should be S. It is, for all five pairs:

08

Transposition costs nothing

_o() transposes each box independently, so there are four combinations to handle. There are not — only one side ever needs flipping. Writing the true relation as Gᵢ = S Hᵢ S⁻¹ and setting Y = Sᵀ X:

box Abox Bflip A?reduces toX
HᵢGᵢnoX Hᵢ = Gᵢ XS
HᵢᵀGᵢyesX Hᵢ = Gᵢ XS
HᵢGᵢᵀyesY Hᵢᵀ = Hᵢᵀ Y ⇒ Y = IS⁻ᵀ
HᵢᵀGᵢᵀnoY Hᵢᵀ = Hᵢᵀ Y ⇒ Y = IS⁻ᵀ

Transposing both boxes is absorbed by the S ↔ S⁻ᵀ symmetry, so a single boolean covers all four cases — and it does not matter which of the two you land on, because _f maps them to the same canonical form.

Key and decryption

10

Why the hook had to be there

The rank-25 seeds are not a slip that can simply be deleted — they are load-bearing. Remove them and every subspace is uniform, the boxes become indistinguishable, and matching a conjugate pair means solving the bilinear subspace-conjugacy problem across 703 candidates per dimension. The author needed a canonical hook for the puzzle to have a solution at all.

The mistake is which hook. A rank anomaly at density 2-47 is visible to anyone who can enumerate the subspace, and m ≤ 18 makes that a fourteen-second scan. Worse, the same hook that separates real boxes from decoys also fixes the correspondence Hᵢ → Gᵢ — the one piece of information that turns the bilinear problem into a linear one. One marker solved all three subproblems at once.

—

Takeaways

  • Ask what survives the obfuscation. _o() looks like it hides the subspace, but a change of basis preserves every element of it. List the quantities invariant under whatever scrambling a scheme applies, then check whether the construction disturbs any of them.
  • A distinguisher and a key recovery are often the same observation. The low-rank elements identified the real boxes, paired them, and pinned the basis correspondence. A marker canonical enough to find is usually canonical enough to exploit.
  • MinRank is only hard when the space is big. Its hardness is asymptotic in the dimension of the span. At m ≤ 18 the whole subspace fits in a Gray-code loop and the security argument evaporates.
  • Verify with the tag, not the plaintext. An AEAD hands you a free proof of correctness — no eyeballing of ASCII required.
